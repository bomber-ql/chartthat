SCQA: the four beats every winning proposal opens with

Field note #8 · 6 min read

Field note #7 said the answer goes first. Fair — but walk into a cold room and announce "you should buy our solution in the South region this quarter" as your literal first sentence, and it lands as arrogance. The pyramid needs a runway. Barbara Minto built that runway too. It's four beats, and once you see them you notice every good pitch — and every good Apple keynote — using them: Situation, Complication, Question, Answer.

S: situation — start inside their world

Open with a statement about the client's reality that is true, stable, and impossible to disagree with. "You operate four regional distribution centers, and the South region has carried 38% of order volume for two years running." That's it. No opinion, no us, no problem. The test of a good Situation: heads nod. You're establishing shared ground — and subtly establishing that you did the reading, so the rest of the room grants you the floor.

This is where a chart can appear early — a quiet, neutral, gray-toned snapshot with nothing highlighted. Its only job is to make the shared reality visible. (Resist the urge to spike it; the highlight comes when the complication does.)

C: complication — the thing that changed

Then introduce the disruption: something changed, broke, opened, or is about to. "Over the last three quarters the South's service level dropped nine points while its volume share kept rising." The Complication creates tension — and here is the subtle part: it must complicate their situation, not yours. Not "our company has a great product" — their world tilts. A good complication makes the client feel the cost of doing nothing. This is where the chart gets its single accent color: the falling line, the one red bar, the widening gap. Same chart family as the Situation, now charged with meaning.

Nobody buys anything while their world feels stable. The complication is the only moment in the deck where demand is created.

Q: question — the one their tension just raised

State the question the complication forces. "So the question is: can the South's service level be recovered without taking capacity from the other three regions?" Say it out loud, on the slide, as one clean sentence. This beat takes ten seconds and beginners skip it — fatal. The explicit question is the hinge of the whole pitch: it hands the room a puzzle they now want solved, and it pre-commits the answer to a decision they've already framed with you. Whatever they were privately worrying about, you've named it; naming a question is halfway to owning the answer.

A: answer — the pyramid lands

"Yes — with a regional sorting retrofit in Q1. Here's the answer, and here are the three reasons it holds." That sentence is the apex of the pyramid from field note #7, and you've arrived there in about ninety seconds with the room already wanting it. Everything after this beat is the three arguments and their chart evidence — proof on demand, not a mystery tour.

Why the order is non-negotiable

Skip S and you pitch to strangers. Skip C and there's no reason to change — stable rooms don't buy. Skip Q and the answer solves a puzzle nobody felt, which is the exact feeling of a generic vendor deck. The four beats map to the client's internal monologue:

  • "That's us." — Situation
  • "…huh, yeah, and that's getting worse." — Complication
  • "So what do we do?" — Question
  • "Right — tell me more." — Answer

If your opening doesn't produce that inner monologue in order, reorder it until it does.

Variations for the room you're in

  • Time-poor room (the CFO who gave you five minutes): compress S and C into one sentence each — "South carries 38% of volume, and its service level is down nine points while the other regions hold." Question. Answer. Thirty seconds.
  • Hostile or skeptical room: put the Complication in their own numbers, neutrally sourced to their dashboard. You can't argue with a chart built from your own data.
  • Renewal / upsell room: the complication is an opportunity, not a threat — "the constraint you planned around last year no longer exists." Same four beats, positive tension.
  • RFP response: their tender document usually hands you S and C. Quote it; they wrote the situation and complication themselves, so your answer arrives as the one that was listening.

One page, four labels

Drafting hack: make your opening slide carry the four words as tiny margin labels — Situation / Complication / Question / Answer — until the deck ships, then delete them. Every opening I've ever seen fail was failing one specific beat, and the labels make the weak one impossible to hide from yourself.

Make their world visible, tilt it, name the question, hand them the answer. The next twenty slides just defend those ninety seconds.
