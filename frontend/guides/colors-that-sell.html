Colors that sell

Field note #4 · 5 min read

I once watched a rival agency get torn apart in a steering committee for a chart that was technically perfect. Every number right, every label present — and every bar painted in our agency's signature orange, in a deck for a bank whose entire identity is deep green. The CFO didn't say a word about data. She said, "This doesn't feel like us." The pitch died of aesthetics. I have never forgotten it.

Color in a client deck is not decoration. It does two jobs at once: it tells the client this is yours, and it tells them look here. Mix the jobs up and the chart sells nothing.

Rule one: the client's palette is the room

Before you make a single chart, open their brand book — or their website — and lift three things: their primary brand color, a dark neutral from it, and a light neutral. The chart panel, the neutral series, the text: all live in those colors. When their own green runs through your slides, the deck stops being a vendor document and becomes their analysis they happen to be watching you present. Ownership transferred before you opened your mouth.

This is thirty seconds of work after export: select all, recolor neutral. It is the cheapest trust on the slide.

Rule two: one accent, and it is never borrowed

Inside that room of neutrals, place exactly one loud color — the single bar, line, or block that carries the claim. Edit two in the previous note exists for a reason: the eye follows saturation, and the eye can follow only one thing at a time.

The accent can come from the client's palette too — a brighter or deeper step of their own brand color works beautifully. Just don't spend it twice. Two loud bars is two competing claims; five loud bars is a confetti cannon, and nobody remembers anything.

Rule three: respect the meanings color already has

Walking into a finance meeting with arbitrary color assignments is like walking into the meeting speaking the wrong language and hoping. In these rooms the vocabulary is fixed:

  • Red means negative. Decline, loss, cost, over budget, risk. Never use red for a hero series that is doing well — half the room will read it as danger before they read the label.
  • Green means positive in most markets (growth, achieved, in budget) — but in China, red is the prosperous, celebratory color and green can carry loss. Know which room you're in; when unsure, use brand blue for "good" and a neutral gray for context.
  • Gray means context. Last year, the benchmark, the others — everything the claim is measured against. Gray says "this exists, it is not the point."

The classic composition writes itself: gray rivals, one colored champion, and red reserved exclusively for the gap you're about to say you can close.

Rule four: color must survive the projector and the printout

Two things always happen that nobody plans for. The conference-room projector washes everything out, and your deck gets printed in black and white for the person who takes notes on paper. So never encode meaning by hue alone. Red/green pairs — "up vs down" — become indistinguishable at 12% grayscale. Add a second channel: shape, direct labels, bold vs light weight, position. Test the chart in grayscale before you ship. If the claim survives without color, the color will only make it stronger.

Rule five: dark decks or light decks, but pick one

Charts pasted from a dark tool into a white master deck look like stickers, and stickers read as "not ours." Match the slide background family before export — light chart for a white deck — or rebuild the slide around the dark chart. A chart belongs to the page it sits on. The ten-second test

Squint at the chart until it blurs. What survives as a single colored shape? That's what the client will remember. If two things survive, delete one color. If the client's identity survives nowhere on the page, start with rule one.

Their palette, your one accent, meaning respected, grayscale-safe. Five rules, one chart.
