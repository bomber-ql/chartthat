When a stacked bar wins

  • The sentence is "the mix is shifting". Same total, different composition — the sliding segments say reallocation better than any table.
  • Total and parts both matter. Budget envelopes, revenue by segment per quarter: the bar top carries the total, the segments carry the structure.
  • Two to four parts. At that count every segment stays readable and the chart stays calm.

When it lets you down

  • You compare the middle segments. Only the bottom segment has a baseline; everything above floats. If the middle is the story, use grouped bars.
  • Five-plus components. The stack turns into geological strata. Group the tail or split the chart.
  • The units differ. Percent stacked on millions is a lie of scale — mixed units belong on a combo chart.

Field notes on stacks

  • Put the segment you are selling at the bottom. It is the only one with a baseline — the only one the eye can truly compare across bars.
  • Same unit, always. A stack is an addition problem; adding dollars to percentages is how credibility leaks.
  • Label the totals on top. The segments explain, the total convinces — give the bar top its number.
  • Order segments by meaning, not by accident. Biggest at the bottom, or the strategic one at the bottom — but never alphabetical.
