When an area chart wins

  • Volume is part of the sentence. "Traffic has doubled" feels bigger when the space under the curve is filled — the area is the accumulation.
  • One series, one hero. A single filled shape is calm and confident. It fills the slide without filling it with ink.
  • The baseline is meaningful. Zero visits, zero stock — when empty actually means empty, the fill to the floor tells the truth.

When it lets you down

  • Several series overlap. Filled shapes hide each other; stacked area charts are readable only in textbooks. Racing series belong on a line chart.
  • Precision matters. People read the top edge, not the area — if exact values are the point, bars win.
  • The zero line is arbitrary. Filling down to a meaningless baseline manufactures drama. That is a line chart with an honest axis.

Field notes on areas

  • Keep the fill translucent. A soft wash under a confident line reads as weight; a solid block reads as a wall.
  • Truncating the axis is riskier here. The filled area implies magnitude — a chopped baseline inflates it. If you cut the axis, say so out loud.
  • Use it for the macro number. Total traffic, total users, total inventory — the series where the accumulation itself is the achievement.
  • Urgency still wants the edge. The slope of the top line carries the argument; the fill is the emphasis, not the message.
