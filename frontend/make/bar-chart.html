When a bar chart wins

  • Ranking is the story. Sorted bars make the leader and the laggard readable from the back of the room — before you say a word.
  • Category names are long. Product names, department names, campaign names. If they start colliding, switch to the horizontal bar chart and let the text breathe.
  • One period, many items. Bars answer "who is bigger right now" better than any other shape.

When it lets you down

  • Time is your axis. Twelve months of revenue as bars forces the eye to jump between tops. That is a line chart's job.
  • More than seven or eight categories. The chart turns into a picket fence. Cut the tail, group it as "Other", or go horizontal.
  • You are showing composition. If the point is how the pie splits, not who wins, use a stacked bar instead.

Field notes on bars

  • Sort descending unless the order itself means something (a funnel, a process). An unsorted bar chart makes the audience do the ranking in their heads — they will not bother.
  • Start the axis at zero. Bars encode value as length; a truncated axis turns a 10% gap into a 200% one, and someone in the room will notice.
  • Spend your color on one bar. Everything in quiet gray-blue, the one bar you are talking about in full color. The chart makes your point before you open your mouth.
  • Label the values, delete the gridlines. Direct labels end the squinting; the gridlines were only there to support the squinting.
